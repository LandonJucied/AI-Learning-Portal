01 / WHAT IS AI?

Understanding Artificial Intelligence

Artificial intelligence is a broad field involving computer systems designed to perform tasks associated with aspects of human intelligence. These tasks can include recognizing patterns, processing information, understanding language, making predictions, and generating content.

AIArtificial Intelligence

The broader field of intelligent computer systems.

MLMachine Learning

A method of learning patterns from data.

NNNeural Networks

Connected computational units that can learn patterns.

05 / MODEL LIMITATIONS

What AI Can—and Cannot—Tell You

An AI output is not the same thing as a verified fact. A system can be useful at recognizing patterns and generating likely responses while still missing context, repeating errors in its training material, or producing an answer that sounds certain but is wrong.

STRENGTH

Pattern recognition

AI can find relationships across large collections of examples that would be difficult to review manually.

LIMITATION

Not guaranteed truth

Fluent wording does not prove that a claim is correct. Verify important facts using reliable sources.

CONTEXT

Input matters

Incomplete, outdated, or unrepresentative data can affect the result a model produces.

06 / CONCEPT LAB

Training, Testing, and Generalization

During training, a machine-learning system adjusts its internal parameters using examples. A separate validation or test set helps evaluate whether it can perform on examples it did not simply memorize.

01

Training set

Examples used to learn patterns.

02

Validation

Checks choices made while developing a model.

03

Test set

Estimates performance on unseen examples.

04

Generalization

How well learned patterns work on new cases.

Key idea

A model that performs well on familiar training examples may still struggle with new situations. Testing matters because real users encounter data the model has not seen before.

07 / REAL-WORLD EXAMPLE

A video or music service may compare viewing history, ratings, and patterns among users to estimate what someone may enjoy. These predictions can be convenient, but they can also narrow what a person sees if the system repeatedly recommends similar material.

Input

Pattern

Output

Human judgment

Decide whether the recommendation is useful and explore beyond it.

08 / UNDER THE HOOD

Neural Networks: Layers, Weights, and Signals

A neural network transforms inputs through connected layers of calculations. During training, adjustable values called weights change so the model's outputs better match the examples or objective it is learning from. The name is inspired by biology, but artificial neural networks are mathematical systems—not miniature human brains.

INPUT

INPUT LAYER

Receives information

Inputs might be numerical measurements, tokens from text, or pixel values from an image.

HIDDEN LAYERS

Transform signals

Learned weights and mathematical operations combine signals into representations.

OUTPUT LAYER

Produces a result

The output could be a class prediction, a score, a next token, or another generated element.

Important distinction

A network's output depends on its training, inputs, design, and task. The diagram is conceptual; real networks can have many more units and layers.

09 / LEARNING APPROACHES

Three Common Ways Models Learn

01

Supervised learning

Training examples include target labels or answers. A model might learn to classify messages as spam or not spam.

EXAMPLE: LABELED EMAILS
02

Unsupervised learning

The system looks for structure in data without a provided target label for every example. It may group similar items.

EXAMPLE: CUSTOMER GROUPS
03

Generative modeling

The system learns patterns that help it produce new content, such as text, images, audio, or code.

EXAMPLE: DRAFTING TEXT

These categories are useful introductions, not an exhaustive taxonomy. Some modern systems combine multiple training approaches.

10 / CASE STUDY

When a Model Memorizes Instead of Learning

Imagine a model trained to recognize dogs using only photos taken on grass. It may perform well on those training photos but fail when a dog appears indoors. The model may have relied on a background shortcut instead of robust features of the animal.

Warning signs

  • Excellent training results but weak test results
  • Performance changes sharply in a new setting
  • Predictions depend on irrelevant clues

Ways to investigate

  • Test on examples from different settings
  • Review which groups and conditions are represented
  • Compare errors rather than only one average score

BIAS & FAIRNESS / THINK CRITICALLY

Where Bias Can Enter

Bias can appear in the examples used to train a system, the way a task is defined, the labels applied to data, or the way a result is interpreted. A system may perform differently for groups that are underrepresented in its data.

01

Ask who is represented

Are the examples broad enough for the intended use?

02

Compare outcomes

Could different groups receive different quality or treatment?

03

Seek context

What information or perspectives are missing?

04

Keep oversight

Use human review for consequential decisions.

Three Responsible-Use Scenarios

SCENARIO A

An AI tool provides a quotation and a book title, but you cannot find the quotation in the book. The responsible action is to leave it out until you verify it from a reliable source.

SCENARIO B

IMPACT REVIEW / FROM PRINCIPLE TO PRACTICE

Assess an AI Use Before Deploying It

Risk depends on the context, the people affected, the information involved, and what happens if the system is wrong. A low-stakes brainstorming tool and a system influencing access to education or employment should not be reviewed in the same way.

Review questionWhat to investigate
Purpose

Is AI needed for this task, and what human goal does it support?

Data

Was the information collected appropriately? Is it relevant, protected, and representative?

Consequences

Who could be excluded, misled, exposed, or harmed if the output is wrong?

Oversight

Who checks the result, handles appeals, and takes responsibility?

CASE STUDY / FAIRNESS AND ACCOUNTABILITY

A Model Performs Differently Across Groups

A school tests an automated tool that sorts student support requests. The overall accuracy looks high, but a review finds that requests written in a dialect less represented in the training examples are more often misclassified.

What not to conclude

  • One overall score proves the system is fair
  • People affected should simply adapt their language
  • Automation removes the need for an appeal process

Responsible next steps

  • Measure performance across relevant groups
  • Investigate data coverage and labeling choices
  • Keep a human review path and document limitations
  • Pause or restrict use until risks are addressed
Ethics is operational

Fairness and accountability require evidence, monitoring, a way to challenge errors, and people who are responsible for decisions—not just a statement of good intentions.

PRIVACY / MINIMIZE EXPOSURE

Share the Minimum Necessary Information

Removing a name does not always make a record anonymous. Details can sometimes be combined to identify a person. Before using an AI tool, remove unnecessary personal details, confirm that the tool is approved for the information, and follow the rules of the school or organization.

Keep out passwords, private student records, confidential documents, and sensitive personal details.Check first tool policies, data retention, permissions, and organizational requirements.Use safer examples fictional or generalized examples when real identifying details are not needed.
